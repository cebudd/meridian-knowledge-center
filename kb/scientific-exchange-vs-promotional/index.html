Scientific Exchange vs. Promotional: a field guide for new MSLs

📄 Owner: Meridian Medical Affairs Compliance 🕐 6 min read 📅 Last updated: April 22, 2026 👁️ 2,184 views

Why this distinction matters

As a Medical Science Liaison, almost every interaction you have with a healthcare professional sits in one of two regulatory buckets: Scientific Exchange or Promotional. The line between them governs what you can say, what materials you can share, what you must document, and — most consequentially — whether your activity exposes Meridian to FDA enforcement.

This article gives you the working mental model, a comparison table to keep handy, examples on both sides, and a clear answer for "what do I do when I'm not sure."

Bottom line up top: if you cannot honestly describe an interaction as non-promotional, scientific, and responsive to an unsolicited request, treat it as promotional until Compliance tells you otherwise.

The two modes

Scientific Exchange

Non-promotional, balanced, scientific dialogue with healthcare professionals about disease state, mechanism of action, clinical evidence, or unapproved data — typically in response to an unsolicited request from the HCP. This is the MSL's primary mode.

Promotional

Communication intended to influence prescribing or recommendation of a specific Meridian product within its approved label. This is the work of Sales, not Medical Affairs. As an MSL, you do not promote.

At-a-glance comparison

Scientific Exchange ✅ Promotional ❌ (for MSLs)
Who initiates HCP, unsolicited Meridian-initiated outreach
Content scope Disease state, mechanism, full data including off-label and pipeline On-label product benefits only
Tone Balanced, presents limitations and contradicting data Selective, emphasizes product strengths

Examples — clearly Scientific Exchange

You're safe here

  • An oncologist emails you asking for the full hazard ratio data from a recent Phase III trial. You send the published paper and offer to discuss.
  • A hematologist at a congress asks how Solvera compares mechanistically to other agents in its class. You walk through the binding-site differences and the supporting in vitro data.
  • A treating physician asks whether there's any signal for a patient population not in the current label. You share the relevant pipeline data, note the lack of approved indication, and answer follow-ups.
  • A KOL invites you to present unpublished real-world evidence at their institutional grand rounds. You present, including limitations and alternative interpretations.

Examples — you've crossed into Promotional

Stop and reset

  • You cold-call an HCP to "share some great new data" on a Meridian product. (Unsolicited from your side, not theirs.)
  • An HCP asks about efficacy, and you respond only with the favorable subgroup analyses without mentioning the failed primary endpoint.
  • You imply, suggest, or recommend that the HCP prescribe a Meridian product for a specific patient.
  • You leave a branded marketing piece behind after a scientific exchange visit.
  • You discuss pricing, formulary placement, market access, or competitive positioning. (These are commercial topics. Refer to your Sales counterpart.)

The gray zones

Real fieldwork is full of edge cases. Here are the ones that come up most often, and how to think about them:

An HCP asks about something you weren't ready to discuss

You can always say "let me get back to you with the full data" and follow up later. You are not required to answer everything on the spot, and pausing to consult Medical Information is always defensible.

An HCP asks if your product is "better" than a competitor

You may present comparative data that has been published in peer-reviewed literature. You may not make subjective superiority claims. If no head-to-head data exists, say so.

Your Sales counterpart asks you to join a customer meeting

Joint visits are permitted when the HCP has requested scientific dialogue. The MSL and Sales rep should clearly separate their interactions — Sales handles promotion, MSL handles scientific exchange. Document both halves separately.

You're asked about an off-label use during a planned promotional event

The Sales rep should excuse themselves. The MSL portion of the conversation should be clearly delineated, documented as unsolicited scientific exchange, and logged in Veeva Medical CRM.

What to do when you're not sure

Default to pause. Saying "great question — let me follow up with you so I can give you a proper answer" is always compliant and almost always appreciated by the HCP. It is never the wrong answer.

  1. Pause the conversation and capture the HCP's question verbatim.
  2. Consult Compliance via the Medical Affairs Compliance helpdesk (live agent during US business hours, 4-hour SLA via Lumen after hours).
  3. Document the question and the resolution in your Veeva call record so the gray-zone interaction is fully auditable.
  4. Follow up with the HCP within Meridian's unsolicited request response standard (typically 5 business days).

Documentation requirements

Every Scientific Exchange interaction must be logged in Veeva Medical CRM within 24 hours with:

  • The HCP's identity and unsolicited request (verbatim, if possible)
  • The topic discussed, drawn from Meridian's Medical Affairs topic library
  • Any materials shared (must be linked from Veeva — do not share off-system)
  • Follow-up commitments with due dates
  • The basis for the interaction being non-promotional

Incomplete or late call records are the single most common Medical Affairs audit finding. They take five minutes — log them.
